## Sheet: Research-Gap Narrative

Research-Gap Narrative

Across the reviewed literature, three things are now well established. First, machine learning and time-series methods can forecast dengue and other notifiable diseases with operationally useful accuracy, but no single algorithm dominates: NNAR led the only nationwide Philippine benchmark (Olana et al., 2025), XGBoost led in Bangladesh and Singapore (Liu et al., 2025; Tian et al., 2024), Random Forest reached 92% outbreak-alert accuracy in Malaysia (Ismail et al., 2022), and Prophet's advantage appeared only at longer horizons (Chen & Moraga, 2025) while losing to ARIMA and LSTM elsewhere (Baker et al., 2025). Second, spatial analysis of Philippine dengue is maturing: provincial scan statistics identify persistent hotspot provinces (Olana et al., 2026), and barangay-level studies in Quezon City and South Cotabato show that hotspots are unstable from year to year (Medina et al., 2023, 2025; Ygoña et al., 2025). Third, digital reporting platforms measurably improve surveillance timeliness in low-resource settings (Ibrahim et al., 2021; Reynolds et al., 2022), and aberration-detection algorithms such as EARS and Farrington can anticipate outbreaks weeks ahead of routine case reporting (Santos-Silva et al., 2025; Truong et al., 2026).

What remains unclear is whether these results transfer to the scale at which Philippine local government units actually act. Every Philippine forecasting study located works on monthly, national or provincial series; none forecasts weekly counts for a single city at the cadence PIDSR prescribes. The barangay hotspot studies are retrospective, aggregate cases to administrative polygons, and stop at description; none implements the case-address, distance-based rule (three or more cases within 200 metres in four weeks) that surveillance units apply by hand, so none can detect clusters that straddle barangay boundaries. Systematic reviews show the gap is general: only 5.2% of 99 dengue prediction models were externally validated (Leung et al., 2023), and interpretability and system integration remain the leading barriers to deployed AI surveillance (Villanueva-Miranda et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2024). Threshold algorithms have been benchmarked on national and metropolitan data but never on the five-year-average baselines PIDSR requires, and geocoding, the step that links case addresses to maps, is nearly absent from the literature; the one recent evaluation found free and commercial services alike achieve low exact-match accuracy on informal addresses (Sanglard et al., 2025).

Further research is therefore necessary on three fronts that existing studies leave disconnected. The forecasting, clustering, threshold-alerting and dashboard strands each have credible evidence in isolation, yet no study integrates them into one system that ingests a live PIDSR registry, geocodes free-text addresses, detects cross-barangay clusters, applies WHO/CDC epidemic thresholds, and forecasts weekly incidence for an LGU. The Philippine evidence base in particular has no city-level, address-resolved, prospective implementation, and no evaluation of whether such a system meets the ISO/IEC 25010 quality expectations of the epidemiologists who would use it. H-MAP addresses this gap in Parañaque City, using 24 years of PIDSR data to test whether methods validated at national scale hold at the scale of a city's weekly surveillance decisions.
